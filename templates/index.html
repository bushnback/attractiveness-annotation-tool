Phase 2
Mixed adaptive strategy — all images seen, now building toward {{ target }} comparisons per image using a smart mix: 60% adaptive (similar BT scores — most informative), 20% least-seen (coverage maintenance), 20% random (bridge-building across score groups).
Phase 3
Extreme-focused refinement — all images at target. Focus shifts to validating the top and bottom zones: 50% boundary (one face from an extreme zone vs one just outside it — tests who belongs), 25% adaptive (similar scores anywhere), 15% within-extreme (both from same extreme — sharpens internal order), 10% random (bridge-building).
Phase 4
Bottom-zone deep refinement — manually activated when you are satisfied with the top zone. Locks the top zone completely (no top-zone face ever appears in a pair) and focuses all effort on ordering the bottom zone correctly.

Why this is needed: Bottom-zone faces that always lose accumulate scores near zero, making them indistinguishable by the adaptive strategy. Without targeted comparisons they remain unordered. Why anchors are used: Comparing only bottom-zone faces against each other causes scores to drift upward — wins accumulate and bad faces start looking average. Anchor comparisons against mid-ranking faces keep the bottom zone correctly positioned on the full scale.

TS Mode
TrueSkill tracks an uncertainty value (σ) for every face and automatically targets the comparisons that will be most informative — especially at the top and bottom of the ranking where accuracy matters most. There are no fixed phases to work through; when the ranking order looks correct to you and σ values are low, you have enough data. How does this work? ▸
Strategy breakdown
35% boundary — one face from an extreme zone (top or bottom), one from the band just outside it. This is the most impactful comparison type: it directly tests whether a face belongs in the top/bottom group or not.
30% within-extreme — both faces from the same extreme zone. Sharpens the internal ordering of the best and worst faces.
20% global uncertainty — any two faces, weighted by σ. Lets the model fill in gaps wherever it is least confident.
15% random — pure random from all images. Higher than usual to prevent "bridge starvation" (see below).
Why boundary comparisons matter most
The faces most likely to be mis-ranked sit right at zone edges — a face just inside the top zone vs one just outside. Getting these boundary decisions right has the biggest impact on whether the final top and bottom groups are correct.
What σ means and why it's better than score-similarity
σ (sigma) is how uncertain the model is about a face's true skill. A high-σ face might jump up or down several ranks with one more comparison. Unlike BT's score-similarity pairing (which picks faces with similar scores), σ-weighting tells the model exactly how much it would learn from seeing a face again — regardless of its current score.
Bridge starvation and the 15% random component
Aggressively targeting zone extremes means mid-ranking faces get very few comparisons. Without occasional "bridge" comparisons across the full range, the model can't calibrate extreme faces against the population. 15% random ensures every face gets some comparisons that anchor the global scale.

Bradley-Terry vs. TrueSkill

Bradley-Terry (BT)

A classical statistical model from 1952 (R.A. Bradley & M.E. Terry) that estimates a single strength parameter for each item from pairwise comparisons. The probability that item A beats item B is modelled as a logistic function of their score difference.

✓ Simple, interpretable scores (0–100)
✓ Well-understood mathematically
✓ Fast to compute
✗ No concept of uncertainty — a face compared 3× looks as confident as one compared 30×
✗ All comparisons weighted equally regardless of how "surprising" the result is

TrueSkill

Developed by Microsoft Research in 2006 (Herbrich, Minka & Graepel) for Xbox matchmaking. Each item has a skill estimate μ (mean) and an uncertainty σ. After every comparison both are updated — surprising results cause bigger updates. The conservative score μ − 3σ is used for ranking.

✓ Tracks confidence — faces seen rarely have wide σ, signalling uncertainty
✓ Surprise-weighted updates converge faster to true ranking
✓ Conservative score naturally penalises under-compared faces
✗ More complex — two parameters per face instead of one
✗ Sensitive to initial σ and draw probability settings
In practice: With enough comparisons the two models tend to converge. TrueSkill is especially useful early on when comparison counts vary widely between faces, since it naturally discounts under-sampled items.
